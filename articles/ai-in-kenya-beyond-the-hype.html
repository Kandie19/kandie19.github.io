STRATEGIC TECHNOLOGY ANALYSIS / AUGUST 2026

AI in Kenya: Beyond the Hype

Building What Matters — from AI adoption to national AI capability.

BY KELVIN KANDIEREVISED EDITION20-PAGE ANALYSISKENYA / AFRICA

Executive Abstract

Artificial intelligence is becoming a general-purpose layer of economic, institutional and infrastructural systems. For Kenya, the strategic question is no longer whether AI will be adopted, but whether adoption will generate durable national capability.

This revised paper argues for strategic autonomy within interdependence: Kenya should use global AI technologies aggressively while deliberately building local capacity in infrastructure, compute, data, technical talent, research, cybersecurity, governance, capital and commercialisation.

CORE PROPOSITION

Kenya should use global AI aggressively while deliberately accumulating the capability to build, adapt, secure, govern, commercialise and retain value from intelligent systems.

1. The Strategic Question Beneath the AI Hype

Artificial intelligence has moved from specialist research into ordinary economic and institutional activity. Generative systems now support language, coding, analysis, image generation, decision support and increasingly complex forms of automation. The speed of this transition has made adoption highly visible. Capability is not.

A chatbot is visible. An AI-enabled workflow is visible. A product demonstration is visible. The less visible layer is where national technological position is actually formed: computing infrastructure, data, research institutions, engineering teams, energy, capital, cybersecurity, intellectual property and institutions capable of evaluating and governing intelligent systems.

Kenya enters this transition with meaningful digital foundations. The country has a National Artificial Intelligence Strategy 2025–2030 and an implementation roadmap. Official sources also confirm a substantial connected-device environment and a relatively favourable AI-preparedness position within Sub-Saharan Africa. The policy architecture matters, but policy documents do not automatically create capability. Implementation is the strategic test.

Kenya should use AI adoption as a mechanism for accumulating capability. The goal is not isolation from global technology. It is the ability to choose where to depend, where to build, where to adapt, where to govern and where to retain economic value.

2. Kenya's Starting Position

Kenya should neither underestimate its position nor exaggerate it. The country is not starting from technological isolation. Its mobile ecosystem, digital financial infrastructure, technology sector, entrepreneurial community and expanding digital services provide a substantial substrate on which AI capability can be built.

Communications Authority of Kenya data show the scale of that substrate. By the end of December 2025, 78.3 million mobile phones were connected to Kenyan networks, equivalent to 149.4 percent device penetration; smartphones accounted for 92.9 percent penetration. These are device measures rather than unique-person measures, so they should not be interpreted as universal digital inclusion. They do, however, demonstrate the depth of Kenya's connected-device environment.

The IMF's AI Preparedness Index gives Kenya a 2023 score of 0.45. The index is a diagnostic of preparedness for AI adoption, not a ranking of invention leadership. Kenya's score therefore supports a limited conclusion: the country has a relatively favourable starting platform within the region, not that it is already an AI leader.

The more useful question is what happens next. A digital foundation becomes national AI capability only when connectivity is connected to compute, data, competence, research, secure deployment, commercialisation and institutions that can learn from implementation.

3. From Adoption to Capability

The distinction between adoption and capability is the paper's central analytical move. Using an AI assistant to draft correspondence is adoption. Embedding machine learning into an operating process is integration. Building an intelligent system around proprietary or lawfully governed data, domain expertise and locally relevant workflows is a deeper form of capability. Developing the research, infrastructure and engineering competence required to improve that system over time is deeper still.

The capability ladder is not intended to imply that every organisation or sector must move linearly through every stage at the same speed. It is a strategic lens for identifying where value, learning and decision-making capacity are accumulating.

Access means people and organisations can reach intelligent systems. Adoption means organisations use AI in existing workflows. Adaptation means systems are localised for language, institutions, industries and operating conditions. Integration means intelligence becomes embedded in productive and institutional infrastructure. Innovation means new methods, systems, products and research outputs emerge. Ownership means meaningful intellectual, economic and strategic value is retained through IP, data assets, expertise, infrastructure, brands, distribution and decision authority.

The revised edition adds a prioritisation test. A national capability intervention should score highly on strategic importance, economic spillover, feasibility, time to impact, private-sector leverage, export potential and security relevance. This does not produce a mechanical ranking; it forces recommendations to explain why they deserve scarce institutional and financial attention.

4. Kenya Should Not Try to Reproduce the Frontier

Kenya's strategy should begin with a strategically liberating proposition: it does not need to win every layer of artificial intelligence. The global AI economy is highly concentrated. The World Bank's 2025 Digital Progress and Trends Report reports that high-income countries accounted for 87 percent of notable AI models, 86 percent of AI start-ups and 91 percent of cumulative venture capital funding in AI start-ups as of 2025.

That concentration makes a simple imitation strategy unattractive. Kenya does not need to build a trillion-dollar frontier-model company to derive substantial economic value from AI. It should instead identify layers where context, engineering, market access or institutional knowledge create leverage.

This does not mean abandoning fundamental research. It means differentiating between areas where frontier competition is strategically justified and areas where adaptation, application, infrastructure, security, data and domain-specific engineering can generate more attainable and durable value.

Strategic principle: do not imitate the frontier; build where context creates leverage. Strategic agency is more realistic than technological autarky.

5. Context as an Economic Asset

AI systems do not operate in a vacuum. A model can be technically sophisticated and still perform poorly when confronted with unfamiliar languages, institutions, workflows, economic structures or operating constraints. Context therefore matters twice: it determines whether a system works, and it can become a source of differentiation when encoded into products and processes.

Kenya already has institutional assets that can support this transition. KALRO maintains national agricultural research infrastructure and repositories. Recent Kenyan agricultural initiatives illustrate the mechanism: satellite data and AI can support crop mapping, crop monitoring, yield prediction and damage detection, while harmonised agronomic data can support site-specific fertilizer recommendations.

The strategic opportunity is to convert domain knowledge and locally relevant data into repeatable research, operational tools, governed datasets, software, intellectual property and eventually exportable products.

Priority domains include agriculture, healthcare, education, public administration and security. The common requirement is not simply a model. It is a system that combines data, workflow, context, uncertainty management, governance and accountable human decision-making.

High-quality, lawfully governed contextual data should be treated as infrastructure capable of supporting research, services and products—not automatically as proprietary national data.

6. The Four Foundations of National AI Capability

CONNECTIVITY

The access layer: affordable bandwidth, reliable enterprise connectivity, cloud access, interoperable systems and productive participation beyond major technology centres.

COMPUTE

A productive input involving accelerators, storage, high-bandwidth networks, cloud platforms and reliable power, with resilient and diversified supply.

CONTEXT

Strategic data capability covering provenance, lawful access, storage, cross-border processing, training permissions, accountability and local relevance.

COMPETENCY

The long-duration foundation: machine learning, statistics, data engineering, distributed systems, cloud, cybersecurity, robotics, HCI, AI safety and systems engineering.

National AI capability can be organised around four interdependent foundations: Connectivity, Compute, Context and Competency. The framework deliberately adds context to the more familiar infrastructure-and-skills conversation because local relevance is not a cosmetic layer; it affects whether systems are useful, safe and economically defensible.

7. Research, Universities and Commercialisation

A university should not merely teach students to use existing AI products. It should be able to take a locally important problem, formulate a research question, assemble evidence, develop and evaluate a system, publish results and, where appropriate, translate findings into a prototype, public-sector application or enterprise.

Kenya's challenge is not a lack of ideas. It is the continuity between research, compute, data, funding, evaluation, procurement and commercialisation. Not every research project should become a start-up, and not every start-up should become a unicorn. A mature ecosystem needs both intellectual infrastructure and commercial innovation.

Policy should therefore create bridges rather than force every research activity into a commercial template. Competitive grants, shared research compute, industry placements, challenge programmes, evaluation facilities and technology-transfer mechanisms can shorten the distance between research and deployment without eliminating basic research.

8. Cybersecurity and Responsible AI Are Productive Capabilities

AI capability without security is incomplete capability. Kenya's expanding digital surface increases the importance of secure model deployment, data protection, identity controls, supply-chain security, monitoring and incident response.

The dual-use character of AI makes this especially important. AI can improve anomaly detection, automation and threat analysis while also increasing attackers' capabilities. Security should therefore be integrated into AI data pipelines, model development, deployment, monitoring and response rather than added after deployment.

Governance should be treated similarly. Risk-based governance, transparent procurement, data protection, model evaluation, human oversight for consequential decisions, auditability, security testing, documentation and institutional expertise are enabling conditions for trustworthy scale. Regulation itself requires technical capacity: a state cannot effectively evaluate technologies it does not understand.

Security and governance are productive capabilities because they determine whether intelligent systems can be deployed repeatedly, at scale, with trust and resilience.

9. Kenya in the African AI Landscape

Kenya should benchmark itself against relevant African ecosystems without turning benchmarking into imitation. The useful comparison is not simply who has more start-ups. It is how countries are assembling policy, talent, data, infrastructure, research and commercialisation.

Rwanda's National AI Policy places emphasis on skills, trusted data, public-sector transformation and responsible private-sector adoption. Nigeria's 2025 National AI Strategy explicitly proposes applied-AI collaboration, international research partnerships, talent-transfer mechanisms and a research-to-commercialisation pathway. South Africa's policy process demonstrates both the value of broad stakeholder engagement and the importance of evidence integrity and policy credibility.

The lesson for Kenya is to learn selectively. Kenya can borrow institutional mechanisms where they fit, avoid expensive symbolic programmes, and differentiate around engineering, domain knowledge, security, regional market access and commercially relevant applications.

10. From Vision to Execution: Seven National Programmes

The strategic argument becomes useful only when it can guide decisions. The seven programmes below are recommendations, not claims that government has already adopted these exact structures. They form a mutually reinforcing portfolio rather than seven unrelated projects.

The portfolio is deliberately sequenced: programmes 1–3 establish enabling foundations; programmes 4–5 deepen research and secure capability; programmes 6–7 convert capability into institutional deployment and regional commercial value.

01 · FOUNDATION

National AI Infrastructure

Objective: Secure resilient access to research and productive compute without assuming full domestic self-sufficiency.

24-month targets: 10+ institutions using shared research compute; a national compute-demand baseline; transparent access-cost reporting; resilience requirements for critical AI workloads.

02 · FOUNDATION

National AI Talent Pipeline

Objective: Move from broad AI literacy to deep technical competence and retention.

24-month targets: 100 advanced fellows or equivalent funded research placements; 25+ industry research placements annually; measurable retention tracking; a national registry of participating research labs.

03 · FOUNDATION

Kenyan Data and Language Initiative

Objective: Create high-quality, representative and lawfully governed datasets for research and products.

24-month targets: A catalogue of 50+ priority datasets; provenance and licensing metadata; dedicated language-data pilots; approved secure data-sharing mechanisms.

04 · CAPABILITY

AI Research and Commercialisation Network

Objective: Connect research to prototype, evaluation, deployment and commercialisation where appropriate.

24-month targets: 20+ research-to-prototype transitions; 10+ independently evaluated pilots; a licensing/technology-transfer pipeline; at least 5 export-oriented reference deployments.

05 · CAPABILITY

Secure AI Programme

Objective: Make security an architectural property of national AI capability.

24-month targets: 50+ high-impact systems assessed or tested; national AI-security guidance; incident-reporting pathways in priority sectors; a specialist-training pipeline.

06 · DEPLOYMENT

Responsible Public-Sector AI

Objective: Make government a sophisticated buyer, evaluator and steward of AI.

24-month targets: A standard AI procurement framework; evaluation of 100% of designated high-impact AI procurements; audited lifecycle controls; trained public-sector AI evaluators.

07 · SCALE

African AI Export Strategy

Objective: Turn Kenyan context and engineering into regional and global commercial value.

24-month targets: 5+ African reference markets; a tracked AI-export revenue pipeline; export-readiness support for 20+ firms; measurable IP retained by Kenyan firms.

11. Financing Capability: Capital Must Match the Technology

Capital is an enabling layer across all seven programmes. The objective should not be to maximise the number of AI start-ups. It should be to increase the probability that technically credible ideas survive long enough to become reliable products, infrastructure or research outputs.

Public funding is most defensible where market incentives are weak but spillovers are high: research, shared compute, standards, public-interest datasets, security research, evaluation infrastructure and foundational training. Private capital is better positioned for scalable products, infrastructure and commercial deployment. The gap can be addressed through blended mechanisms, challenge funds, procurement commitments, matching grants and patient capital.

Procurement should be treated as a technology-development instrument. A sophisticated buyer can specify performance, security, interoperability, data governance, lifecycle management and measurable outcomes. Procurement can therefore create demand for capability rather than demand for hype.

12. Ownership and Strategic Autonomy

Ownership should be defined more precisely than national control of every technological component. A realistic conception includes at least six dimensions: intellectual-property ownership, data governance and asset control, specialised engineering capability, infrastructure and operational control, economic value capture, and decision authority.

Strategic autonomy is therefore different from self-sufficiency. A Kenyan company may rely on foreign accelerators, cloud infrastructure or foundation models while still owning its product IP, customer relationships, domain datasets, engineering expertise and commercial distribution. Conversely, a system can be hosted locally while remaining strategically dependent on external software, models, skills or vendors.

The objective should be strategic autonomy within interdependence: enough capability to understand dependencies, negotiate them, diversify critical inputs, switch providers where feasible and retain meaningful economic and strategic value.

13. Measuring Whether Capability Is Actually Accumulating

Kenya should complement adoption metrics with a national AI capability scorecard. The scorecard should be treated as a management instrument rather than a prestige ranking.

A useful national question is not “How many people use AI?” but “Which capabilities are stronger today than they were two years ago, and can we demonstrate the improvement?”

14. Two Possible Kenyas

Measures usage and productivity gains.Measures usage plus capability accumulation, IP, retention and export value.
Government is primarily a buyer.Government is a sophisticated buyer, evaluator and steward.
Data remains mostly an input.Governed contextual data becomes research and product infrastructure.
External dependency can deepen unnoticed.Dependencies are mapped, diversified and negotiated.
Value is captured mainly through adoption.Value is captured through products, IP, expertise, infrastructure and regional exports.

Trajectory One is Kenya as an exceptional consumer of global AI. Businesses adopt foreign platforms rapidly. Professionals become highly skilled users. Government deploys imported systems. Productivity rises. This trajectory could generate substantial economic value and should not be dismissed. Its strategic limitation is dependence.

Trajectory Two is Kenya as a capability builder. It remains an active adopter of global AI while simultaneously developing research institutions, engineering teams, data assets, secure infrastructure, specialised systems, sophisticated procurement and export-oriented firms.

Kenya can be excellent at consuming intelligence built elsewhere, or it can become excellent at converting intelligence into local capability. The second trajectory does not reject the first; it builds on it.

15. What Kenya Should Do Differently

The strategic shift is from catching up to positioning. Kenya should ask where local context matters, where global markets are underserved, where African data can create legitimate value, where Kenyan engineering can compete, where research can generate IP and where regional scale can make specialised products economically viable.

The African market is particularly important. A system designed for Kenyan agricultural conditions may be useful elsewhere in East Africa. A language technology system built around East African languages may have regional relevance. A logistics system designed around African transport networks may scale across borders. A secure-intelligence product designed for complex African operating environments may find markets beyond the continent.

Strategic progression: Kenya as market → Kenya as development base → Kenya as African technology hub → Kenya as global contributor. The bridge between those stages is capability.

16. Limitations and Research Agenda

This paper does not estimate AI's causal contribution to Kenya's GDP, forecast precise employment effects, or establish the returns to individual policy interventions. Its recommendations are strategic and normative.

The seven-programme portfolio and the 24-month targets in this revised edition are planning proposals, not government commitments, fiscal forecasts or predictions. They are intended to make the strategy testable and to create a basis for future empirical work.

Priority research should examine firm-level AI productivity, sector and firm-size adoption, dataset availability and quality, national compute demand, labour-market transitions, research-commercialisation returns, public-sector AI outcomes, AI security incidents and Kenya's position in African AI value chains.

17. Conclusion: Build What Matters

Kenya has a credible starting platform for the AI era: digital connectivity, an established technology ecosystem, research institutions, entrepreneurial capacity and a national AI policy framework. None of these guarantees leadership.

The next phase requires capability: the ability to build, research, compute, secure, govern, finance, commercialise and retain value from intelligent systems. This does not require technological isolation. It requires deliberate choices about dependencies and domestic strengths.

The most important shift is therefore not from “non-AI” to “AI”. It is from passive consumption to capability accumulation. Kenya should continue using the world's best AI systems. It should also build the institutions, data assets, engineering expertise, research capacity, secure infrastructure and companies capable of adapting those systems and creating new ones.

The strategic question is no longer how quickly Kenya can adopt artificial intelligence. It is what Kenya must build today to shape the AI future it wants tomorrow. The answer should be measured not by the volume of AI hype, but by the accumulation of capability.
